How we think about this

A contract is never finished. Keeping it alive is the job.

Every business, every agency, every program runs on contracts — and no contract is ever done. Laws get passed. Lawsuits get filed. New situations appear that nobody wrote language for, because you can’t contract for what you don’t yet know. So the agreement keeps evolving, and someone has to negotiate each new iteration, hold the line on the terms that matter, and make sure the operation underneath actually delivers what was promised.

That is the work we do: the negotiation, the evolving language, and the operations that turn a signed agreement into something that functions.

The market, honestly

Most organizations have people who handle contracts. So why is this work still sent out?

It’s a fair question — and the answer is the whole opportunity. Companies, agencies, and health plans all employ people who handle contracts, yet they keep sending this work out to specialists and interim help. Not because the wheel needs reinventing, but because of five gaps their permanent staff can’t or aren’t allowed to close.

Capacity

Permanent staff are maxed out. New programs, acquisitions, and renewal cycles spike the workload faster than existing headcount can clear it.

Headcount freezes

Budgets allow contractor spend but not new full-time hires. So the work goes out as a contract or SOW instead of a job posting — same work, different bucket.

Vacancy & interim

A contracting lead leaves. They need six to twelve months of experienced coverage while they recruit a permanent replacement.

Finite projects

A network build, a delegation audit, a procurement cycle — real work with an end date that doesn’t justify a permanent hire.

Specialized expertise

A complex RFP, delegated credentialing, NCQA/URAC readiness, a network build — niche work their generalist contracting staff often don’t have.

That gap — the overflow, the vacancies, the specialized and time-boxed pieces — is exactly where an outside contractor or SME earns their fee. It’s where we come in.

Why now

Whatever’s being rebuilt, it’s made of contracts.

Markets shift, programs launch, deals get renegotiated — and each one is a stack of agreements someone has to negotiate and operations someone has to stand up. California healthcare is a vivid case: CalAIM, Proposition 1, and the Behavioral Health Services Act are remaking who delivers care and how they’re contracted, right now. But the pattern is everywhere, at exactly the moment organizations have the least slack to handle it in-house.

People who can both negotiate the contract and run the operation underneath it are scarce. That is the combination we bring.
